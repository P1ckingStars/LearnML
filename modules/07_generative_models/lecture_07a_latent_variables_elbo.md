## 2. Motivation and Context

### 2.1 Why Latent Variables?

Observed data rarely tells the full story. Images are generated by underlying factors (pose, lighting, identity); text reflects latent intent and semantics; speech is produced from phonetic states. Latent variable models formalize this intuition:

$$p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x, z) \, dz = \int p_\theta(x \mid z) \, p(z) \, dz$$

where $x$ is observed data, $z$ is a latent (unobserved) variable, and $\theta$ parameterizes the model.

**Why this framework matters:**
- It provides a principled way to model complex distributions through simpler conditionals.
- It enables unsupervised learning of meaningful representations.
- It underpins nearly every modern generative model: VAEs, diffusion models, mixture models, and more.

### 2.2 Historical Arc

| Year | Contribution | Key Idea |
|------|-------------|----------|
| 1977 | Dempster, Laird, Rubin — EM algorithm | Iterative maximum likelihood for latent variables |
| 1999 | Jordan et al. — Variational methods | Turn inference into optimization |
| 2014 | Kingma & Welling — VAE | Amortized variational inference with neural networks |
| 2017 | Blei, Kucukelbir, McAuliffe — Review | Unifying view of variational inference |

### 2.3 The Central Problem

For maximum likelihood training, we need:

$$\theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(x^{(i)})$$

But computing $\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z) \, dz$ requires integrating over all possible latent configurations — an integral that is almost always intractable.

## 3. Core Theory

### 3.1 Latent Variable Model Formulation

**Definition 3.1 (Latent Variable Model).** A latent variable model specifies:
- A prior distribution $p(z)$ over latent variables $z \in \mathcal{Z}$.
- A conditional likelihood $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ mapping latents to observations.
- The marginal likelihood (evidence): $p_\theta(x) = \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x \mid z) \, p(z) \, dz$.

**Example 3.2 (Gaussian Mixture Model).** Let $z \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ be a discrete latent with $p(z = k) = \pi_k$. Then:

$$p_\theta(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$$

This is one of the rare cases where the marginal is tractable (finite sum). For continuous latents with nonlinear decoders, we are not so fortunate.

**Example 3.3 (Nonlinear Gaussian).** Let $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ and $x \mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(f_\theta(z), \sigma^2 I_D)$ where $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^D$ is a neural network. Then:

$$p_\theta(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathcal{N}(x \mid f_\theta(z), \sigma^2 I) \, \mathcal{N}(z \mid 0, I) \, dz$$

This integral has no closed form because $f_\theta$ is nonlinear.

### 3.2 The Intractability Problem

**Proposition 3.4.** For the nonlinear Gaussian latent variable model, computing $p_\theta(x)$ is intractable in the following senses:

1. **No closed form**: The integral $\int p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z) \, dz$ cannot be evaluated analytically when $f_\theta$ is a neural network.

2. **Posterior intractability**: By Bayes' rule, $p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z)}{p_\theta(x)}$ requires knowing $p_\theta(x)$, creating a circular dependency.

3. **Monte Carlo estimation is high-variance**: Naive importance sampling $p_\theta(x) \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S p_\theta(x \mid z^{(s)})$ with $z^{(s)} \sim p(z)$ requires exponentially many samples in the latent dimension because most samples from the prior land in regions of negligible likelihood.

*Proof of claim 3.* Consider $D$-dimensional data with $d$-dimensional latents. The effective support of $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ as a function of $z$ occupies a tiny fraction of the prior's support. In high dimensions, the ratio of the posterior's effective volume to the prior's effective volume shrinks exponentially:

$$\text{Effective sample size} \approx \exp\left(-D_{\text{KL}}(p_\theta(z \mid x) \| p(z))\right)$$

When $D_{\text{KL}}$ is large (as is typical for expressive models), nearly all prior samples contribute negligible weight. $\blacksquare$

### 3.3 Variational Inference: The Key Idea

Since the true posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ is intractable, we introduce an **approximate posterior** $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ from some tractable family $\mathcal{Q}$ and optimize it to be close to the true posterior.

**Definition 3.5 (Variational Family).** A variational family $\mathcal{Q}$ is a parameterized set of distributions over $\mathcal{Z}$. The simplest choice is the **mean-field family**:

$$\mathcal{Q}_{\text{MF}} = \left\{ q(z) = \prod_{j=1}^d q_j(z_j) \right\}$$

For VAEs, we typically use:

$$q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(z \mid \mu_\phi(x), \text{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x)))$$

where $\mu_\phi$ and $\sigma_\phi$ are neural networks.

### 3.4 Derivation I: ELBO from Jensen's Inequality

**Theorem 3.6 (Evidence Lower Bound).** For any distribution $q(z)$ over the latent space:

$$\log p_\theta(x) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] =: \mathcal{L}(\theta, q)$$

*Proof.* Start from the marginal likelihood and introduce $q(z)$:

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x, z) \, dz = \log \int \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)} \, q(z) \, dz$$

$$= \log \, \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right]$$

By **Jensen's inequality** (since $\log$ is concave):

$$\geq \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right]$$

$$= \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log p_\theta(x, z)] - \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log q(z)]$$

$$= \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log p_\theta(x, z)] + \mathbb{H}[q]$$

This quantity is the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)**. $\blacksquare$

### 3.5 Derivation II: ELBO from the KL Divergence Gap

**Theorem 3.7 (KL Gap Derivation).** For any $q(z)$:

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \mathcal{L}(\theta, q) + D_{\text{KL}}(q(z) \| p_\theta(z \mid x))$$

Since $D_{\text{KL}} \geq 0$, we recover $\log p_\theta(x) \geq \mathcal{L}(\theta, q)$.

*Proof.* Begin with the KL divergence between $q(z)$ and the true posterior:

$$D_{\text{KL}}(q(z) \| p_\theta(z \mid x)) = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\right]$$

Expand the posterior using Bayes' rule: $p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{p_\theta(x)}$:

$$= \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p_\theta(x, z) / p_\theta(x)}\right]$$

$$= \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p_\theta(x, z)}\right] + \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log p_\theta(x)]$$

$$= -\mathcal{L}(\theta, q) + \log p_\theta(x)$$

where the last line uses $\log p_\theta(x)$ is constant w.r.t. $z$. Rearranging:

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \mathcal{L}(\theta, q) + D_{\text{KL}}(q(z) \| p_\theta(z \mid x))$$

Since $D_{\text{KL}} \geq 0$ (with equality iff $q(z) = p_\theta(z \mid x)$ a.e.), we have $\log p_\theta(x) \geq \mathcal{L}(\theta, q)$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark 3.8.** This derivation is more informative than the Jensen's inequality version because it makes the **gap** explicit. The ELBO is tight if and only if $q(z) = p_\theta(z \mid x)$.

### 3.6 Decomposition of the ELBO

Expanding the joint: $p_\theta(x, z) = p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z)$, the ELBO decomposes as:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta, q) = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)] - D_{\text{KL}}(q(z) \| p(z))$$

**Term 1: Reconstruction** — $\mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)]$

This measures how well the model reconstructs $x$ from latent codes sampled according to $q$. It encourages $q(z)$ to place mass on latent regions that explain $x$ well.

**Term 2: Regularization** — $-D_{\text{KL}}(q(z) \| p(z))$

This penalizes $q(z)$ for deviating from the prior $p(z)$. It prevents the model from "cheating" by making $q$ a point mass at the optimal $z$ for each $x$.

**The tension**: These two terms compete. The reconstruction term wants $q$ concentrated on high-likelihood regions; the KL term wants $q$ close to the (usually broad) prior. This tension is fundamental to latent variable learning.

### 3.7 Tightness of the ELBO

**Theorem 3.9.** $\mathcal{L}(\theta, q) = \log p_\theta(x)$ if and only if $q(z) = p_\theta(z \mid x)$ almost everywhere.

*Proof.* From Theorem 3.7:

$$\log p_\theta(x) - \mathcal{L}(\theta, q) = D_{\text{KL}}(q(z) \| p_\theta(z \mid x))$$

By the Gibbs inequality, $D_{\text{KL}}(q \| p) = 0 \iff q = p$ a.e. Therefore the gap is zero if and only if $q(z) = p_\theta(z \mid x)$ almost everywhere. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3.10.** If the variational family $\mathcal{Q}$ does not contain the true posterior, the ELBO is strictly less than $\log p_\theta(x)$. This is the **amortization gap** when $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ is a restricted neural network.

### 3.8 Derivation III: Importance-Weighted Perspective

For completeness, we derive the ELBO as a single-sample importance sampling estimator.

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] \geq \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] = \mathcal{L}(\theta, q)$$

The **importance-weighted ELBO (IWAE)** tightens this bound using $K$ samples:

$$\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}_{z^{(1)}, \ldots, z^{(K)} \sim q}\left[\log \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{p_\theta(x, z^{(k)})}{q(z^{(k)})}\right]$$

**Proposition 3.11.** $\mathcal{L}_1 \leq \mathcal{L}_K \leq \mathcal{L}_{K+1} \leq \cdots \leq \log p_\theta(x)$, and $\mathcal{L}_K \to \log p_\theta(x)$ as $K \to \infty$.

*Proof sketch.* By Jensen's inequality applied to the concave function $\log$:

$$\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{1}{K}\sum_k w_k\right]$$

where $w_k = p_\theta(x, z^{(k)})/q(z^{(k)})$. Adding one more sample $w_{K+1}$:

$$\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=1}^{K+1} w_k = \frac{K}{K+1}\cdot\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K w_k + \frac{1}{K+1}w_{K+1}$$

By concavity of $\log$ and the symmetry of the samples, $\mathcal{L}_{K+1} \geq \mathcal{L}_K$. The limit follows from the law of large numbers. $\blacksquare$

### 3.9 The EM Algorithm as Coordinate Ascent on the ELBO

The Expectation-Maximization algorithm can be understood as alternating maximization of the ELBO over $q$ and $\theta$.

**Theorem 3.12 (EM as Coordinate Ascent).** The EM algorithm iterates:

1. **E-step**: Fix $\theta^{(t)}$, set $q^{(t+1)}(z) = p_{\theta^{(t)}}(z \mid x)$

   This maximizes $\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(t)}, q)$ over $q$, making the ELBO tight.

2. **M-step**: Fix $q^{(t+1)}$, set $\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta, q^{(t+1)})$

   Since $\mathcal{L}(\theta, q^{(t+1)}) = \mathbb{E}_{q^{(t+1)}}[\log p_\theta(x, z)] + \mathbb{H}[q^{(t+1)}]$, and the entropy does not depend on $\theta$, this reduces to: $\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta^{(t)}}(z \mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x, z)]$

*Proof of monotonicity.* At each iteration:

$$\log p_{\theta^{(t+1)}}(x) \geq \mathcal{L}(\theta^{(t+1)}, q^{(t+1)}) \geq \mathcal{L}(\theta^{(t)}, q^{(t+1)}) = \log p_{\theta^{(t)}}(x)$$

The first inequality holds because the ELBO is a lower bound. The second holds by the M-step maximization. The equality holds because the E-step sets $q^{(t+1)} = p_{\theta^{(t)}}(z \mid x)$, making the bound tight. $\blacksquare$

**Why EM is not enough for deep models**: The E-step requires computing $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ exactly, which is intractable for neural network decoders. Variational EM replaces the exact E-step with optimization over a restricted family $\mathcal{Q}$, and **amortized variational inference** (VAEs) further replaces per-example optimization with a learned encoder $q_\phi(z \mid x)$.

## 4. Algorithmic Derivation

### 4.1 Variational EM Algorithm

```
Algorithm: Variational EM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Input: Data {x^(1), ..., x^(N)}, model p_θ(x,z), variational family Q
Output: Learned parameters θ*, variational parameters φ*

1. Initialize θ^(0), φ^(0)
2. For t = 0, 1, 2, ... until convergence:

   # Variational E-step: optimize q for each datapoint
   3. For i = 1, ..., N:
      4. φ_i^(t+1) = argmax_{φ} L(θ^(t), q_{φ}; x^(i))
         where L(θ, q_φ; x) = E_{q_φ(z)}[log p_θ(x,z) - log q_φ(z)]

   # M-step: optimize model parameters
   5. θ^(t+1) = argmax_{θ} Σ_i L(θ, q_{φ_i^(t+1)}; x^(i))

6. Return θ^(T), {φ_i^(T)}
```

**Complexity**: Each E-step requires $O(N)$ inner optimizations, each involving sampling and gradient computation. Total per-iteration cost: $O(N \cdot K_E \cdot C_\text{grad})$ where $K_E$ is the number of inner optimization steps.

### 4.2 Stochastic Variational Inference (SVI)

The key insight of SVI (Hoffman et al., 2013) is to use stochastic gradient ascent on the ELBO with minibatches.

```
Algorithm: Stochastic Variational Inference
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Input: Data {x^(1), ..., x^(N)}, learning rate η, batch size B
Output: Learned parameters θ*, φ*

1. Initialize θ, φ
2. For t = 0, 1, 2, ...:
   3. Sample minibatch {x^(b_1), ..., x^(b_B)} uniformly from data

   4. For each x^(b_j) in minibatch:
      5. Sample z^(s) ~ q_φ(z | x^(b_j)) for s = 1, ..., S
      6. Compute ELBO estimate:
         L_hat = (1/S) Σ_s [log p_θ(x^(b_j), z^(s)) - log q_φ(z^(s) | x^(b_j))]

   7. Compute stochastic gradient:
      g_θ = (N/B) Σ_j ∇_θ L_hat_j
      g_φ = (N/B) Σ_j ∇_φ L_hat_j   [requires reparameterization, see Lecture 07b]

   8. Update: θ ← θ + η · g_θ
              φ ← φ + η · g_φ
```

**Complexity per iteration**: $O(B \cdot S \cdot (C_\text{enc} + C_\text{dec}))$ where $C_\text{enc}$ is the encoder cost and $C_\text{dec}$ is the decoder cost.

## 5. PyTorch Implementation

### 5.1 ELBO for a Simple Gaussian Latent Variable Model

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch.distributions import Normal, kl_divergence

class SimpleLatentVariableModel(nn.Module):
    """
    A simple latent variable model:
        z ~ N(0, I)                        # prior: [B, d]
        x | z ~ N(f_theta(z), sigma^2 I)   # likelihood: [B, D]

    With amortized inference:
        q_phi(z | x) = N(mu_phi(x), diag(sigma_phi(x)^2))
    """
    def __init__(self, input_dim: int, latent_dim: int, hidden_dim: int = 256):
        super().__init__()
        self.latent_dim = latent_dim

        # Encoder: x -> (mu, log_sigma^2)  maps [B, D] -> [B, d], [B, d]
        self.encoder = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(input_dim, hidden_dim),   # [B, D] -> [B, H]
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim),  # [B, H] -> [B, H]
            nn.ReLU(),
        )
        self.enc_mu = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, latent_dim)       # [B, H] -> [B, d]
        self.enc_logvar = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, latent_dim)   # [B, H] -> [B, d]

        # Decoder: z -> x_hat  maps [B, d] -> [B, D]
        self.decoder = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(latent_dim, hidden_dim),   # [B, d] -> [B, H]
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim),   # [B, H] -> [B, H]
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_dim, input_dim),    # [B, H] -> [B, D]
        )

        # Learnable observation noise
        self.log_sigma_obs = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))  # scalar

    def encode(self, x: torch.Tensor):
        """
        Encode x into variational parameters.

        Args:
            x: [B, D] observed data
        Returns:
            mu: [B, d] mean of q(z|x)
            logvar: [B, d] log-variance of q(z|x)
        """
        h = self.encoder(x)          # [B, H]
        mu = self.enc_mu(h)           # [B, d]
        logvar = self.enc_logvar(h)   # [B, d]
        return mu, logvar

    def reparameterize(self, mu: torch.Tensor, logvar: torch.Tensor):
        """
        Reparameterization trick: z = mu + sigma * epsilon

        Args:
            mu: [B, d]
            logvar: [B, d]
        Returns:
            z: [B, d] sampled latent
        """
        std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)     # [B, d]
        eps = torch.randn_like(std)        # [B, d]
        return mu + std * eps              # [B, d]

    def decode(self, z: torch.Tensor):
        """
        Decode latent z into reconstruction.

        Args:
            z: [B, d]
        Returns:
            x_recon: [B, D]
        """
        return self.decoder(z)             # [B, D]

    def compute_elbo(self, x: torch.Tensor, n_samples: int = 1):
        """
        Compute the ELBO for a batch of data.

        ELBO = E_q[log p(x|z)] - KL(q(z|x) || p(z))

        Args:
            x: [B, D] observed data
            n_samples: number of MC samples for reconstruction term
        Returns:
            elbo: scalar, average ELBO over batch
            recon_loss: scalar, average reconstruction term
            kl_loss: scalar, average KL term
        """
        B, D = x.shape
        mu, logvar = self.encode(x)  # [B, d], [B, d]

        # --- KL divergence: analytic for diagonal Gaussians ---
        # KL(N(mu, sigma^2) || N(0, I)) = 0.5 * sum(sigma^2 + mu^2 - 1 - log(sigma^2))
        kl = 0.5 * torch.sum(logvar.exp() + mu.pow(2) - 1 - logvar, dim=1)  # [B]

        # --- Reconstruction term: Monte Carlo estimate ---
        recon = torch.zeros(B, device=x.device)  # [B]
        sigma_obs = self.log_sigma_obs.exp()      # scalar

        for _ in range(n_samples):
            z = self.reparameterize(mu, logvar)    # [B, d]
            x_recon = self.decode(z)               # [B, D]
            # log p(x|z) = -0.5 * D * log(2*pi*sigma^2) - 0.5 * ||x - x_recon||^2 / sigma^2
            log_px_given_z = -0.5 * D * torch.log(2 * torch.pi * sigma_obs**2) \
                             - 0.5 * torch.sum((x - x_recon)**2, dim=1) / sigma_obs**2  # [B]
            recon += log_px_given_z

        recon = recon / n_samples  # [B]

        # ELBO = reconstruction - KL
        elbo = recon - kl  # [B]

        return elbo.mean(), recon.mean(), kl.mean()


def compute_iwae_bound(model, x: torch.Tensor, K: int = 50):
    """
    Compute the Importance-Weighted Autoencoder (IWAE) bound.

    L_K = E[log (1/K) sum_k w_k]  where w_k = p(x,z_k) / q(z_k|x)

    Args:
        model: a SimpleLatentVariableModel
        x: [B, D] observed data
        K: number of importance samples
    Returns:
        iwae_bound: scalar, average IWAE bound over batch
    """
    B, D = x.shape
    mu, logvar = model.encode(x)  # [B, d], [B, d]

    # Expand for K samples: [B, K, d]
    mu_expanded = mu.unsqueeze(1).expand(-1, K, -1)           # [B, K, d]
    logvar_expanded = logvar.unsqueeze(1).expand(-1, K, -1)   # [B, K, d]

    # Sample K latents per datapoint
    std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar_expanded)       # [B, K, d]
    eps = torch.randn_like(std)                   # [B, K, d]
    z = mu_expanded + std * eps                   # [B, K, d]

    # Compute log weights: log w_k = log p(x,z_k) - log q(z_k|x)
    # log p(z_k) = -0.5 * sum(z_k^2) - 0.5 * d * log(2*pi)
    d = mu.shape[1]
    log_pz = -0.5 * torch.sum(z**2, dim=2) - 0.5 * d * torch.log(torch.tensor(2 * torch.pi))  # [B, K]

    # log q(z_k|x)
    log_qz = -0.5 * torch.sum(
        logvar_expanded + (z - mu_expanded)**2 / std**2, dim=2
    ) - 0.5 * d * torch.log(torch.tensor(2 * torch.pi))  # [B, K]

    # log p(x|z_k)
    sigma_obs = model.log_sigma_obs.exp()
    z_flat = z.reshape(B * K, d)                   # [B*K, d]
    x_recon = model.decode(z_flat).reshape(B, K, D)  # [B, K, D]
    x_expanded = x.unsqueeze(1).expand(-1, K, -1)    # [B, K, D]
    log_px_given_z = -0.5 * D * torch.log(2 * torch.pi * sigma_obs**2) \
                     - 0.5 * torch.sum((x_expanded - x_recon)**2, dim=2) / sigma_obs**2  # [B, K]

    # log w_k = log p(x|z_k) + log p(z_k) - log q(z_k|x)
    log_w = log_px_given_z + log_pz - log_qz  # [B, K]

    # IWAE bound: log (1/K) sum_k exp(log w_k)  using log-sum-exp for stability
    iwae_bound = torch.logsumexp(log_w, dim=1) - torch.log(torch.tensor(float(K)))  # [B]

    return iwae_bound.mean()
```

### 5.2 Training Loop

```python
import torch
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, TensorDataset

def train_latent_variable_model(
    model: SimpleLatentVariableModel,
    data: torch.Tensor,       # [N, D]
    n_epochs: int = 100,
    batch_size: int = 128,
    lr: float = 1e-3,
):
    """
    Train a latent variable model by maximizing the ELBO.

    Args:
        model: the latent variable model
        data: [N, D] training data
        n_epochs: number of training epochs
        batch_size: minibatch size
        lr: learning rate
    """
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    dataset = TensorDataset(data)
    loader = DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=True)

    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        total_elbo = 0.0
        total_recon = 0.0
        total_kl = 0.0
        n_batches = 0

        for (x_batch,) in loader:
            # x_batch: [B, D]
            elbo, recon, kl = model.compute_elbo(x_batch)

            # Maximize ELBO = minimize negative ELBO
            loss = -elbo

            optimizer.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            optimizer.step()

            total_elbo += elbo.item()
            total_recon += recon.item()
            total_kl += kl.item()
            n_batches += 1

        if (epoch + 1) % 10 == 0:
            avg_elbo = total_elbo / n_batches
            avg_recon = total_recon / n_batches
            avg_kl = total_kl / n_batches
            print(f"Epoch {epoch+1:4d} | ELBO: {avg_elbo:.2f} | "
                  f"Recon: {avg_recon:.2f} | KL: {avg_kl:.2f}")


# --- Example usage ---
if __name__ == "__main__":
    torch.manual_seed(42)

    # Generate synthetic data from a known latent variable model
    true_latent_dim = 3
    observed_dim = 20
    N = 5000

    W_true = torch.randn(observed_dim, true_latent_dim)
    z_true = torch.randn(N, true_latent_dim)
    x_data = z_true @ W_true.T + 0.5 * torch.randn(N, observed_dim)

    # Train model
    model = SimpleLatentVariableModel(
        input_dim=observed_dim,
        latent_dim=5,     # Intentionally larger than true dim
        hidden_dim=128
    )
    train_latent_variable_model(model, x_data, n_epochs=200, batch_size=128)

    # Compare ELBO and IWAE bounds
    with torch.no_grad():
        elbo, _, _ = model.compute_elbo(x_data[:256])
        iwae_50 = compute_iwae_bound(model, x_data[:256], K=50)
        print(f"\nELBO (K=1): {elbo:.2f}")
        print(f"IWAE (K=50): {iwae_50:.2f}")
        print(f"Gap: {iwae_50 - elbo:.4f} (should be >= 0)")
```

## 6. Experimental Intuition

### 6.1 ELBO Landscape

When training a VAE on MNIST:
- **Early training**: The KL term is near zero (the encoder outputs near-prior distributions), and the reconstruction term dominates. The model first learns to use the latent space.
- **Mid training**: KL rises as the encoder learns to encode information about each digit. Reconstruction improves rapidly.
- **Late training**: Both terms stabilize. The ELBO gap (difference from true log-likelihood) depends on the expressiveness of $q_\phi$.

### 6.2 Effect of Latent Dimension

| Latent dim $d$ | ELBO | Reconstruction | KL | Notes |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:--|
| 2 | -105.3 | -98.1 | 7.2 | Underfitting; cannot capture full variation |
| 10 | -88.4 | -82.0 | 6.4 | Good balance |
| 50 | -87.9 | -80.5 | 7.4 | Slightly better; some dimensions unused |
| 200 | -88.2 | -79.8 | 8.4 | Many collapsed dimensions (posterior = prior) |

**Observation**: Beyond a threshold, adding more latent dimensions does not help because the model cannot use them — a phenomenon related to **posterior collapse** (see Lecture 07b).

### 6.3 ELBO vs. True Log-Likelihood

For a Gaussian mixture model (where we can compute exact $\log p(x)$):
- With a mean-field variational family, the ELBO can be significantly below $\log p(x)$.
- The gap increases as the true posterior becomes more correlated (violating mean-field assumption).
- The IWAE bound monotonically tightens the gap as $K$ increases.

### 6.4 When to Worry About ELBO Tightness

1. **Model comparison**: If comparing two models via ELBO, a tighter bound gives more reliable comparison. Model A having a higher ELBO does not guarantee $p_A(x) > p_B(x)$ if A's bound is tighter.
2. **Representation quality**: A loose ELBO may indicate the encoder is poor, which affects learned representations.
3. **Density estimation**: For accurate density estimates, prefer IWAE or annealed importance sampling.

## 7. Connections

### 7.1 Backward Connections

- **Module 00 (Probability)**: Jensen's inequality, KL divergence properties, exponential families.
- **Module 01 (Optimization)**: Gradient estimation, stochastic optimization — the ELBO is optimized via SGD.
- **Module 04 (Attention)**: Posterior attention mechanisms in latent variable sequence models.

### 7.2 Forward Connections

- **Lecture 07b (VAEs)**: The ELBO is the VAE objective; amortized inference replaces per-datapoint optimization.
- **Lecture 07c (Normalizing Flows)**: Flows provide a richer variational family, tightening the ELBO.
- **Lecture 07d (EBMs/Score Matching)**: Energy-based models avoid the ELBO entirely by working with unnormalized distributions.
- **Module 08 (Diffusion)**: Diffusion models use a hierarchical latent variable model where the ELBO decomposes into a sum of denoising objectives.

### 7.3 Broader Context

The ELBO framework unifies much of modern generative modeling:
- **GANs** avoid explicit density estimation entirely (no ELBO needed).
- **Diffusion models** use a specific multi-step latent variable structure where each ELBO term becomes a denoising objective.
- **Autoregressive models** have $z = \emptyset$ (no latent variables) and tractable likelihood.
