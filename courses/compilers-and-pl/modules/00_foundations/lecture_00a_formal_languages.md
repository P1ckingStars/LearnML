## 1. Alphabets, Strings, and Languages

### 1.1 Basic Definitions

**Definition 1.1 (Alphabet).** An *alphabet* $\Sigma$ is a finite, non-empty set of symbols.

**Definition 1.2 (String).** A *string* (or *word*) over $\Sigma$ is a finite sequence of symbols from $\Sigma$. The *empty string* is denoted $\varepsilon$. The set of all strings over $\Sigma$ is denoted $\Sigma^*$, and $\Sigma^+ = \Sigma^* \setminus \{\varepsilon\}$.

**Definition 1.3 (Length).** The *length* of a string $w$, written $|w|$, is the number of symbols in $w$. By convention, $|\varepsilon| = 0$.

**Definition 1.4 (Concatenation).** For strings $x, y \in \Sigma^*$, the *concatenation* $x \cdot y$ (often written $xy$) is the string formed by appending $y$ to $x$. Concatenation is associative with identity $\varepsilon$.

**Proposition 1.5.** $(\Sigma^*, \cdot, \varepsilon)$ is a free monoid generated by $\Sigma$.

### 1.2 Languages and Operations

**Definition 1.6 (Language).** A *language* $L$ over $\Sigma$ is any subset $L \subseteq \Sigma^*$.

**Definition 1.7 (Language operations).** Let $L_1, L_2 \subseteq \Sigma^*$.

- **Union:** $L_1 \cup L_2 = \{w \mid w \in L_1 \text{ or } w \in L_2\}$
- **Concatenation:** $L_1 \cdot L_2 = \{xy \mid x \in L_1, y \in L_2\}$
- **Kleene star:** $L^* = \bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty} L^i$, where $L^0 = \{\varepsilon\}$ and $L^{i+1} = L \cdot L^i$
- **Complement:** $\overline{L} = \Sigma^* \setminus L$
- **Intersection:** $L_1 \cap L_2$
- **Difference:** $L_1 \setminus L_2$

## 2. Regular Languages and Regular Expressions

### 2.1 Regular Expressions

**Definition 2.1 (Regular Expression).** Over alphabet $\Sigma$, the set of *regular expressions* is defined inductively:

1. $\emptyset$ is a regular expression denoting the empty language $\{\}$.
2. $\varepsilon$ is a regular expression denoting $\{\varepsilon\}$.
3. For each $a \in \Sigma$, $a$ is a regular expression denoting $\{a\}$.
4. If $r_1$ and $r_2$ are regular expressions, then $(r_1 | r_2)$, $(r_1 \cdot r_2)$, and $(r_1^*)$ are regular expressions denoting $L(r_1) \cup L(r_2)$, $L(r_1) \cdot L(r_2)$, and $L(r_1)^*$ respectively.

**Algebraic identities.** Regular expressions satisfy the following (where $\equiv$ denotes language equality):

$$r | s \equiv s | r \quad \text{(commutativity of union)}$$

$$r | (s | t) \equiv (r | s) | t \quad \text{(associativity of union)}$$

$$r \cdot (s \cdot t) \equiv (r \cdot s) \cdot t \quad \text{(associativity of concatenation)}$$

$$r \cdot (s | t) \equiv r \cdot s \ |\ r \cdot t \quad \text{(right distributivity)}$$

$$\varepsilon \cdot r \equiv r \cdot \varepsilon \equiv r \quad \text{(identity for concatenation)}$$

$$r^* \equiv \varepsilon \ |\ r \cdot r^* \quad \text{(unfolding of Kleene star)}$$

### 2.2 Arden's Lemma

**Lemma 2.2 (Arden's Lemma).** Let $A, B \subseteq \Sigma^*$ with $\varepsilon \notin A$. Then the equation $X = AX \cup B$ has the unique solution $X = A^* B$.

*Proof.* First, verify $A^*B$ is a solution:

$$A(A^*B) \cup B = A^+ B \cup B = (A^+ \cup \{\varepsilon\})B = A^*B.$$

For uniqueness, suppose $X$ is any solution. We show $X = A^*B$ by showing containment in both directions.

($\supseteq$): Since $X = AX \cup B$, we have $B \subseteq X$. Then $AB \subseteq AX \subseteq X$, and by induction $A^n B \subseteq X$ for all $n \geq 0$. Hence $A^*B \subseteq X$.

($\subseteq$): Suppose $w \in X$ with $|w| = n$. If $w \in B$, then $w \in A^*B$. If $w \in AX$, then $w = av$ for some $a \in A$ (with $|a| \geq 1$ since $\varepsilon \notin A$) and $v \in X$ with $|v| < n$. By strong induction on $|w|$, $v \in A^*B$, so $w \in A \cdot A^*B = A^+B \subseteq A^*B$. $\blacksquare$

## 3. Deterministic Finite Automata (DFAs)

### 3.1 Definition

**Definition 3.1 (DFA).** A *deterministic finite automaton* is a 5-tuple $M = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F)$ where:

- $Q$ is a finite set of states,
- $\Sigma$ is a finite input alphabet,
- $\delta: Q \times \Sigma \to Q$ is the transition function,
- $q_0 \in Q$ is the start state,
- $F \subseteq Q$ is the set of accept (final) states.

**Definition 3.2 (Extended transition function).** $\hat{\delta}: Q \times \Sigma^* \to Q$ is defined by:

$$\hat{\delta}(q, \varepsilon) = q, \qquad \hat{\delta}(q, wa) = \delta(\hat{\delta}(q, w), a).$$

**Definition 3.3.** The language *recognized* by $M$ is $L(M) = \{w \in \Sigma^* \mid \hat{\delta}(q_0, w) \in F\}$.

---

## 4. Nondeterministic Finite Automata (NFAs)

### 4.1 Definition

**Definition 4.1 (NFA).** A *nondeterministic finite automaton* is a 5-tuple $N = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F)$ where $\delta: Q \times \Sigma \to \mathcal{P}(Q)$.

**Definition 4.2 ($\varepsilon$-NFA).** An *$\varepsilon$-NFA* extends the NFA by allowing transitions on $\varepsilon$: $\delta: Q \times (\Sigma \cup \{\varepsilon\}) \to \mathcal{P}(Q)$.

**Definition 4.3 ($\varepsilon$-closure).** For a state $q$, define:

$$\text{ECLOSE}(q) = \{p \in Q \mid p \text{ is reachable from } q \text{ via zero or more } \varepsilon\text{-transitions}\}.$$

This extends to sets: $\text{ECLOSE}(S) = \bigcup_{q \in S} \text{ECLOSE}(q)$.

### 4.2 Extended Transition for $\varepsilon$-NFA

$$\hat{\delta}(q, \varepsilon) = \text{ECLOSE}(q)$$

$$\hat{\delta}(q, wa) = \text{ECLOSE}\!\left(\bigcup_{p \in \hat{\delta}(q,w)} \delta(p, a)\right)$$

## 5. Equivalence of DFAs and NFAs: Subset Construction

### 5.1 The Construction

**Theorem 5.1 (Rabin-Scott, 1959).** For every NFA $N = (Q_N, \Sigma, \delta_N, q_0, F_N)$, there exists a DFA $D = (Q_D, \Sigma, \delta_D, d_0, F_D)$ such that $L(D) = L(N)$.

*Construction.* Define the *subset construction* (also called the *powerset construction*):

- $Q_D = \mathcal{P}(Q_N)$ (each DFA state is a subset of NFA states)
- $d_0 = \text{ECLOSE}(\{q_0\})$
- $\delta_D(S, a) = \text{ECLOSE}\!\left(\bigcup_{q \in S} \delta_N(q, a)\right)$ for $S \subseteq Q_N$, $a \in \Sigma$
- $F_D = \{S \in Q_D \mid S \cap F_N \neq \emptyset\}$

### 5.2 Proof of Correctness

*Proof.* We prove by induction on $|w|$ that $\hat{\delta}_D(d_0, w) = \hat{\delta}_N(q_0, w)$ (interpreting the NFA's extended transition as returning a set of states).

**Base case** ($w = \varepsilon$): $\hat{\delta}_D(d_0, \varepsilon) = d_0 = \text{ECLOSE}(\{q_0\}) = \hat{\delta}_N(q_0, \varepsilon)$. Holds.

**Inductive step** ($w = xa$): Assume $\hat{\delta}_D(d_0, x) = \hat{\delta}_N(q_0, x)$. Let $S = \hat{\delta}_N(q_0, x)$.

$$\hat{\delta}_D(d_0, xa) = \delta_D(\hat{\delta}_D(d_0, x), a) = \delta_D(S, a) = \text{ECLOSE}\!\left(\bigcup_{q \in S} \delta_N(q, a)\right) = \hat{\delta}_N(q_0, xa).$$

Therefore $w \in L(D) \iff \hat{\delta}_D(d_0, w) \in F_D \iff \hat{\delta}_N(q_0, w) \cap F_N \neq \emptyset \iff w \in L(N)$. $\blacksquare$

### 5.3 Complexity

The subset construction can produce up to $2^{|Q_N|}$ states. This bound is tight:

**Theorem 5.2.** For every $n \geq 1$, there exists an NFA with $n$ states such that any equivalent DFA has at least $2^n$ states.

*Example.* Consider the language $L_n = \{w \in \{0,1\}^* \mid \text{the } n\text{th symbol from the end is } 1\}$. An NFA with $n+1$ states recognizes $L_n$, but any DFA requires at least $2^n$ states (provable via Myhill-Nerode).

## 6. Pumping Lemma for Regular Languages

### 6.1 Statement and Proof

**Theorem 6.1 (Pumping Lemma).** If $L$ is a regular language, then there exists a constant $p \geq 1$ (the *pumping length*) such that every string $w \in L$ with $|w| \geq p$ can be written as $w = xyz$ where:

1. $|y| \geq 1$ (the "pump" is non-empty),
2. $|xy| \leq p$ (the pump occurs within the first $p$ symbols),
3. For all $i \geq 0$, $xy^i z \in L$.

*Proof.* Let $M = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F)$ be a DFA recognizing $L$ with $|Q| = p$. Let $w = a_1 a_2 \cdots a_m$ with $m \geq p$.

Consider the sequence of states $q_0, q_1, \ldots, q_m$ where $q_j = \hat{\delta}(q_0, a_1 \cdots a_j)$. Among the first $p+1$ states $q_0, q_1, \ldots, q_p$, by the Pigeonhole Principle there exist $0 \leq i < j \leq p$ with $q_i = q_j$.

Set $x = a_1 \cdots a_i$, $y = a_{i+1} \cdots a_j$, $z = a_{j+1} \cdots a_m$.

- $|y| = j - i \geq 1$.
- $|xy| = j \leq p$.
- For any $k \geq 0$: $\hat{\delta}(q_0, xy^k z)$ ends in $q_m \in F$ because the loop from $q_i$ back to $q_i = q_j$ can be traversed any number of times. $\blacksquare$

### 6.2 Using the Pumping Lemma

The pumping lemma is used to prove languages are *not* regular via contradiction. The proof structure is an adversarial game:

1. Assume $L$ is regular (for contradiction).
2. Let $p$ be the pumping length (chosen by the adversary).
3. Choose a specific $w \in L$ with $|w| \geq p$.
4. For *all* decompositions $w = xyz$ satisfying conditions (1) and (2), show that there exists $i \geq 0$ such that $xy^i z \notin L$.

**Example.** $L = \{a^n b^n \mid n \geq 0\}$ is not regular.

*Proof.* Assume $L$ is regular with pumping length $p$. Choose $w = a^p b^p$. For any decomposition $w = xyz$ with $|xy| \leq p$ and $|y| \geq 1$, we have $y = a^k$ for some $k \geq 1$. Then $xy^0z = a^{p-k}b^p \notin L$ since $p - k \neq p$. Contradiction. $\blacksquare$

## 7. The Myhill-Nerode Theorem

### 7.1 Equivalence Relations and Right Invariance

**Definition 7.1.** For a language $L \subseteq \Sigma^*$, define the *Myhill-Nerode equivalence relation* $\equiv_L$ on $\Sigma^*$:

$$x \equiv_L y \iff (\forall z \in \Sigma^*)(xz \in L \leftrightarrow yz \in L).$$

This relation is:
- An equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive).
- *Right invariant*: $x \equiv_L y$ implies $xa \equiv_L ya$ for all $a \in \Sigma$.
- A *refinement of $L$*: $L$ is a union of equivalence classes.

### 7.2 The Theorem

**Theorem 7.2 (Myhill-Nerode).** The following are equivalent:

1. $L$ is recognized by some DFA.
2. $L$ is the union of some equivalence classes of a right-invariant equivalence relation of finite index on $\Sigma^*$.
3. $\equiv_L$ has finite index (i.e., finitely many equivalence classes).

Moreover, the number of equivalence classes of $\equiv_L$ equals the number of states in the *minimum-state DFA* for $L$.

*Proof sketch.* $(1 \Rightarrow 2)$: Given DFA $M$, define $x \sim_M y \iff \hat{\delta}(q_0, x) = \hat{\delta}(q_0, y)$. This is right-invariant, has index $\leq |Q|$, and $L$ is a union of classes.

$(2 \Rightarrow 3)$: Any right-invariant equivalence relation $\sim$ of finite index that is a refinement of $L$ refines $\equiv_L$. Hence $\text{index}(\equiv_L) \leq \text{index}(\sim) < \infty$.

$(3 \Rightarrow 1)$: Construct DFA $M_L = (Q_L, \Sigma, \delta_L, [\ \varepsilon\ ], F_L)$ where $Q_L = \Sigma^* / \equiv_L$ (the equivalence classes), $\delta_L([x], a) = [xa]$ (well-defined by right invariance), and $F_L = \{[x] \mid x \in L\}$. Then $L(M_L) = L$, and $M_L$ has minimum states. $\blacksquare$

### 7.3 Applications

- **Minimum DFA uniqueness:** The minimum-state DFA for a regular language $L$ is unique up to isomorphism (it is the quotient automaton $M_L$).
- **Proving non-regularity:** If one can exhibit infinitely many pairwise $\equiv_L$-distinguishable strings, then $L$ is not regular. This is often cleaner than the pumping lemma.

## 8. Context-Free Languages and Grammars

### 8.1 Context-Free Grammars

**Definition 8.1 (CFG).** A *context-free grammar* is a 4-tuple $G = (V, \Sigma, R, S)$ where:

- $V$ is a finite set of variables (nonterminals),
- $\Sigma$ is a finite set of terminals (disjoint from $V$),
- $R \subseteq V \times (V \cup \Sigma)^*$ is a finite set of production rules,
- $S \in V$ is the start symbol.

A production $(A, \alpha) \in R$ is written $A \to \alpha$.

**Definition 8.2 (Derivation).** We write $\alpha A \beta \Rightarrow \alpha \gamma \beta$ if $A \to \gamma$ is a production. The reflexive-transitive closure $\Rightarrow^*$ gives derivation in zero or more steps. The *language generated* by $G$ is:

$$L(G) = \{w \in \Sigma^* \mid S \Rightarrow^* w\}.$$

**Definition 8.3.** A *leftmost derivation* always expands the leftmost nonterminal. A *rightmost derivation* always expands the rightmost nonterminal.

### 8.2 Parse Trees and Ambiguity

**Definition 8.4 (Parse tree).** A parse tree for $G$ is an ordered, rooted tree where:
- The root is labeled $S$.
- Each internal node is labeled with a nonterminal $A$, and its children (left to right) correspond to the right-hand side of some production $A \to X_1 X_2 \cdots X_k$.
- Each leaf is labeled with a terminal or $\varepsilon$.

**Theorem 8.5.** $w \in L(G)$ if and only if there exists a parse tree for $w$ in $G$. Moreover, there is a bijection between leftmost derivations and parse trees.

**Definition 8.6 (Ambiguity).** A CFG $G$ is *ambiguous* if there exists $w \in L(G)$ with two or more distinct parse trees (equivalently, distinct leftmost derivations). A CFL $L$ is *inherently ambiguous* if every CFG generating $L$ is ambiguous.

**Example.** $L = \{a^i b^j c^k \mid i = j \text{ or } j = k\}$ is inherently ambiguous (Parikh, 1966).

### 8.3 Normal Forms

**Theorem 8.7 (Chomsky Normal Form).** Every CFL not containing $\varepsilon$ can be generated by a CFG in which every production has the form $A \to BC$ or $A \to a$, where $A, B, C \in V$ and $a \in \Sigma$.

**Theorem 8.8 (Greibach Normal Form).** Every CFL not containing $\varepsilon$ can be generated by a CFG in which every production has the form $A \to a \alpha$ where $a \in \Sigma$ and $\alpha \in V^*$.

## 9. Pushdown Automata

### 9.1 Definition

**Definition 9.1 (PDA).** A *pushdown automaton* is a 7-tuple $P = (Q, \Sigma, \Gamma, \delta, q_0, Z_0, F)$ where:

- $Q$ is a finite set of states,
- $\Sigma$ is the input alphabet,
- $\Gamma$ is the stack alphabet,
- $\delta: Q \times (\Sigma \cup \{\varepsilon\}) \times \Gamma \to \mathcal{P}_{\text{fin}}(Q \times \Gamma^*)$ is the transition function,
- $q_0 \in Q$ is the start state,
- $Z_0 \in \Gamma$ is the initial stack symbol,
- $F \subseteq Q$ is the set of accept states.

### 9.2 Acceptance Modes

- **Accept by final state:** $L_F(P) = \{w \mid (q_0, w, Z_0) \vdash^* (q, \varepsilon, \gamma) \text{ for some } q \in F, \gamma \in \Gamma^*\}$.
- **Accept by empty stack:** $L_\varepsilon(P) = \{w \mid (q_0, w, Z_0) \vdash^* (q, \varepsilon, \varepsilon) \text{ for some } q \in Q\}$.

**Theorem 9.2.** The two acceptance modes are equivalent: a language is accepted by final state by some PDA iff it is accepted by empty stack by some PDA.

### 9.3 Equivalence with CFGs

**Theorem 9.3.** A language is context-free if and only if it is accepted by some PDA.

*Proof outline.* CFG $\to$ PDA: Given $G = (V, \Sigma, R, S)$, construct a PDA that simulates leftmost derivations. Push $S$ onto the stack. If top is nonterminal $A$, nondeterministically choose production $A \to \alpha$ and replace $A$ with $\alpha$. If top is terminal $a$, match it against current input symbol.

PDA $\to$ CFG: More involved. For PDA $P$, construct a grammar whose variables are triples $[q, A, p]$ representing "starting in state $q$ with $A$ on top of the stack, the PDA can reach state $p$ having popped $A$." $\blacksquare$

## 10. The Chomsky Hierarchy

The Chomsky hierarchy classifies grammars (and hence languages) into four types:

| Type | Grammar Restriction | Automaton | Language Class |
|------|-------------------|-----------|----------------|
| 0 | Unrestricted ($\alpha \to \beta$, $\lvert\alpha\rvert \geq 1$) | Turing machine | Recursively enumerable |
| 1 | Context-sensitive ($\alpha A \beta \to \alpha \gamma \beta$, $\lvert\gamma\rvert \geq 1$) | Linear bounded automaton | Context-sensitive |
| 2 | Context-free ($A \to \gamma$) | Pushdown automaton | Context-free |
| 3 | Regular ($A \to aB$ or $A \to a$) | Finite automaton | Regular |

**Strict containment:** $\text{REG} \subsetneq \text{CFL} \subsetneq \text{CSL} \subsetneq \text{RE}$.

### 10.1 Key Separating Results

- $\{a^n b^n\} \in \text{CFL} \setminus \text{REG}$ (pumping lemma).
- $\{a^n b^n c^n\} \in \text{CSL} \setminus \text{CFL}$ (CFL pumping lemma).
- The halting problem language is in $\text{RE} \setminus \text{CSL}$.

### 10.2 Closure Properties Summary

| Property | REG | CFL | CSL | RE |
|----------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Union | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Intersection | Yes | **No** | Yes | Yes |
| Complement | Yes | **No** | Yes | **No** |
| Concatenation | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Kleene star | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Intersection with regular | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

## 11. Pumping Lemma for Context-Free Languages

**Theorem 11.1 (CFL Pumping Lemma).** If $L$ is a context-free language, then there exists a pumping length $p$ such that every $w \in L$ with $|w| \geq p$ can be written as $w = uvxyz$ where:

1. $|vy| \geq 1$,
2. $|vxy| \leq p$,
3. For all $i \geq 0$, $uv^i x y^i z \in L$.

*Proof.* Let $G$ be a CNF grammar for $L$ with $|V| = k$ variables. Set $p = 2^k$. Any parse tree for $w$ with $|w| \geq p$ has height $> k$. On the longest root-to-leaf path, there are $> k$ nonterminals, so some nonterminal $A$ repeats. The two occurrences of $A$ define the decomposition: the subtree rooted at the upper $A$ yields $vxy$; the subtree at the lower $A$ yields $x$. Pumping corresponds to repeating or removing the "annular" region between the two $A$-subtrees. $\blacksquare$

## 12. Decision Problems for Regular and Context-Free Languages

### 12.1 Regular Languages

All of the following are decidable for regular languages (given as DFAs):

- **Membership:** Is $w \in L$? -- $O(|w|)$
- **Emptiness:** Is $L = \emptyset$? -- Reachability from $q_0$ to $F$, $O(|Q| + |\delta|)$
- **Universality:** Is $L = \Sigma^*$? -- Check $\overline{L} = \emptyset$
- **Equivalence:** Is $L_1 = L_2$? -- Check $(L_1 \setminus L_2) \cup (L_2 \setminus L_1) = \emptyset$
- **Inclusion:** Is $L_1 \subseteq L_2$? -- Check $L_1 \cap \overline{L_2} = \emptyset$

### 12.2 Context-Free Languages

| Problem | Decidable? |
|---------|-----------|
| Membership | Yes (CYK: $O(n^3)$) |
| Emptiness | Yes |
| Universality | **No** (undecidable) |
| Equivalence | **No** (undecidable) |
| Ambiguity | **No** (undecidable) |
| Intersection emptiness | **No** (undecidable for two CFLs) |
